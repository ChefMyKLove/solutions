The Chef

Coding Confidential

Thirty years in professional kitchens teaches you things no bootcamp curriculum can even mention: how a burn on the forearm barely registers by hour six; or the way you can read a cook's entire day just by how they're gripping the knife at 4pm… steady hands mean the ticket times are holding, shaky ones mean a customer's special night out is about to go horribly wrong. You learn that "the ticket's not done till the plate's right" isn't a mantra taped above an expo station for team morale. It's the only law that matters, because the alternative is serving something that will haunt you the rest of service — or maybe the rest of your career.

I didn't walk away from that life, though there were times when I thought I couldn't take it any longer. No, it walked away from me through an injury that the hero I thought I was could just power through. Making it a career-ending injury, ending a three-decade run with zero ceremony. One day you're running the line of a Michelin quality kitchen. The next you're struggling to keep up when you should have just taken a week off, and your hand is slowly becoming crippled as you keep trying to show up like you know you should be able to…

But can't any longer.

Here's what I didn't expect through the two-plus years since that day: it's that I wasn't actually starting over. I was just switching mediums. Cooking was never the whole picture, it was one instrument in a much bigger practice. I've been making things with my hands my whole life: soap, sound, fibre, paint, poems, stories… confection… Cooking just happened to be the one that paid rent for thirty years. So when the kitchen closed, I didn't lose my identity. I lost access to one canvas and picked up another.

That new canvas turned out to be code. A bootcamp full of twenty-somethings and one very confused former chef, learning to debug JavaScript with the same part of my brain that used to rescue a broken hollandaise mid-service. Nobody in either industry tells you it's the same job: recipe development and debugging are both transformation under pressure — raw material, forced through enough iterations, until it becomes something that actually works for somebody. Mise en place is sprint planning with fewer knives and worse burns. Systems thinking, holding the whole operation in your head at once, reading a room before it boils over — that's not a skill I picked up in a course. That's fifteen years of expo talking through my commits now.

I also do something most developers won't touch: I go into other people's builds and fix what's broken, finish what got abandoned half-cooked. Every kitchen I ever ran, I inherited somebody else's mess at some point: a walk-in nobody organized, or a menu that wasn't costed out properly. Your job isn't to complain about it though. You're there to fix it and keep service moving. Same instinct applies to a codebase somebody left in a bad state. I'm not precious about whose name is on the original commit. I care whether the thing works and whether it lasts.

Which is the part that never changes, no matter the medium: a line cook does not plate a dish and vanish. You watch it go out. You watch it come back. You fix what's wrong before the table has to say a word. So when I ship a build, either mine or someone else's I've taken over, it comes with real support after launch. Not a sales tactic. The same discipline that never let me walk away from a plate mid-service, and never will let me walk away from your code.

I'm not interested in software that just works, same as I was never interested in food that was just edible. Kitchens taught me that most systems, culinary or digital, are built to exclude somebody. I spent thirty years trying to make sure the people on my line got fed and got paid fairly. I'm spending this decade doing the same thing with code — and with soap, sound, rainbows, and stories on the side, because apparently one art form was never going to be enough to hold everything I've got to say.

If you want to see what happens when the cooking and the code and the art all land on the same plate at once, go look at naked.chefmyklove.com. Five bucks gets you the browser-based edition — a piece built the same way I build everything else now: by hand, from scratch, with no shortcuts and nothing served half-finished.
